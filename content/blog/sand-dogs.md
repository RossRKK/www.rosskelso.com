What sand dogs tell us about society.

<!-- more -->

Many beggars partake in some kind of busking or street art in order to entice
people into giving them money, and they are not called scammers. Just today I
saw a homeless woman in the centre of Oxford drawing a kite on the pavement with
chalk. She is not a scammer for doing this.

Over the past couple of days I have been reminded of a phenomenon which was once
very familiar to me: men on the street making sculptures of dogs made out of
sand. This was a common sight on Buchanan and Sauchiehall Streets when I used to
live in Glasgow.

Recently I started to see videos online about these sand dogs which fall into 2
camps:[^camps]

1. people appreciating the dogs at face value
2. sand dog truthers

By which I mean people who are certain that the sculptures are fake. They
present this argument in anonymous comments and videos accosting the street
performers[^accost] but only provide hearsay to back up their claims. I have
appointed myself the task of determining whether or not it is true that these
sand artists are the real deal or scammers.

First of all: what do people mean when they say that these men are "scammers"? I
posit that they mean that the people who make these dogs did not put any effort
into their craft and are essentially begging and using the impressiveness of a
skill they do not actually possess to solicit donations.

It is not, in any case, a very good scam, in that it doesn't really "dupe" you:
if you're unimpressed by the artwork — which the people calling it a scam
presumably are — you simply don't contribute.

The larger issue that I see though is that based on a slightly sceptical and
over curious reading of the evidence commonly presented to me online[^sample]
there is no actual evidence that this is a scam.

Many people in the comments of these videos claim that dog sculptures are made
using moulds. This is not a new idea.[^origin] There are 2 ways I've seen
suggested that this might work:

1. the mould forms some internal structure which they just place some extra sand
   on top of to create the illusion of a sand sculpture
2. they fill a mould with sand and lift it off to form the shape of the
   sculpture

The first option can be tested on the spot, and destructively, by shoving
something into the sculpture to see if it collapses (or otherwise behaves sand
like). The second is harder, because once the shape has been formed the mould
can be spirited away and hidden. To settle that one you have to watch the thing
being built.

I have found no affirmative evidence of these sculptures being set up using a
mould. The closest anyone gets is anonymous testimony in the comments of
Waywell's post.[^anon] Given this we will assess the issue based on the evidence
we do have.

The first option goes quickly. I have seen at least one video of a person
accosting one of these guys saying it's a scam and deliberately pressing his
finger into the sculpture. The sand moved as you would expect for a real sand
sculpture.[^poke] Juliet Robertson has done the same test more politely, on two
different dogs in two cities, and written it up.[^edinburgh]

The second takes longer, but videos exist showing the sculptures in various
states of construction. In one several dog heads are finished while the bodies
behind them are still amorphous blobs of sand. Better still, Robertson also has
a photograph of an Edinburgh pitch in which the dog is not yet a dog at all:
just a heap of sand tipped onto a cloth, next to the bags it came out
of.[^midbuild] It makes sense that the head would be built first as it is the
cute part and presumably the money maker, and if there was a mould it would
presumably make the entire sculpture at once rather than head first.
Demonstrations of how to build these dogs are readily available online.[^demo]

The fact that they are often similar and always dogs does lend credence to the
idea that they are a mould due to the repetition. However, I think this can
easily be explained by the fact that dogs will perform well due to their
cuteness, and once an artist has a technique for creating a sculpture that
performs well they are likely to repeat it. Not to mention the way dogs lie is
conducive to a structure made from sand. Furthermore no two of the sculptures I
have seen are exactly the same[^variety] and if a single mould were being used
you would expect them to be (or to be drawn from a smallish pool).

The reason I think this is interesting enough to write a blog post about is the
irony. The sand artist is only supposedly dishonest, and there is at least some
evidence that he isn't. The commenter accusing him, asserting something he has
no evidence for, actually is dishonest (if only intellectually).

Clearly the sculptures are real: when poked they move like compacted wet sand —
not something solid; they have been spotted and documented under construction;
no "sand dog truther" has ever provided any evidence of a mould being used other
than hearsay. It's not that unbelievable that a man can make a sculpture of a
dog from sand given a little practice.

What can we learn from this? I think "sand dog sculptures are fake" might be the
world's lowest stakes conspiracy theory. It has no large scale impact on the
world but it has the same shape as the real ones: a claim adopted without
evidence because it fits a story you already believe. It is too easy for a claim
to go unsuspected simply because it feels right.

People have a right to be sceptical and I think that is healthy. Just as I have
a right to be sceptical of the scepticism. Although I have now spent an evening
gathering citations about pavement dogs in order to be the man who saw through
the men who saw through it. Make of that what you will.
